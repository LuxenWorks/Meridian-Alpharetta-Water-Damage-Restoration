What makes one wet room a bigger job than the next

Two houses can both say my floor is wet and need very different work. Before describing a plan, a contractor weighs a handful of things.

Distance comes first: did the water stay on the surface, or soak into padding, baseboards and the wall behind them? Then time. EPA's mold guide wants wet areas and items dry in 24 to 48 hours, so the hours already passed matter. Then the starting point: the CDC links floodwater and sewage to germs, so water that came up through a drain needs more care than a spill from a supply line. Last is the material, since carpet, drywall, insulation and wood dry differently, and drywall that cannot be disinfected gets thrown away, the CDC says.

None of that shows from the doorway, so the first visit is for looking and measuring. Read the guides to practice the looking before anyone arrives.

Five things people ask with water on the floor

Where does my wet room land on a 1-to-10 check?

Give what you see a number. An 8 to 10 is sparks or a burning smell, water around outlets or cords, sewage, a ceiling that sags, or a gas smell: get out and use 911 or the utility first. For gas, Atlanta Gas Light is 877.427.4321. A 4 to 7 is a room that is wet and still spreading, a this-week problem. At the 1 to 3 end sits a damp patch to dry and watch; still damp next morning, call it a 4.

Can I switch the power off myself?

Only from dry ground. Standing in water while switching power on or off, or running an electric tool, is what the CDC's flooded-home page warns against. If the main switch can be reached only by wading in, that job belongs to an electrician, and the CDC wants wiring checked before power returns. Sparks, buzzing or a burning smell mean leave the house and use 911 from outside.

How long before mold becomes a worry?

Two EPA pages frame it. The mold guide wants water-damaged areas and items dry in 24 to 48 hours. The flood-cleanup page names wood, drywall, carpet and furniture as the materials that can grow mold when they stay wet past 24 hours, and says drying usually takes several days to weeks. Neither page predicts one particular house. If carpet or drywall is still wet after the first day, call (678) 929-5373.

Which photos help with an insurance claim?

The 2020 storm tips from Georgia's insurance commissioner's office list four things: a written record and photographs of the damage, receipts kept, a call to your insurer, and significant repairs left to others. Photograph every room first, before anything moves, and again as items come out. Only your own insurer can say what a policy pays. The office's consumer line is 1-800-656-2298.

Who answers for a water main or sewer line in the street?

That depends on the town. For North Fulton, Fulton County lists 770-640-3040 for drinking water emergencies and 404-612-3061 for sewer. Forsyth County Water and Sewer lists 770-781-2160 for after-hours emergencies. Part of Roswell runs its own water utility, so a recent bill shows who supplies you. Water already inside the house is a different call.

A city mostly built in the 1990s

The 2024 five-year survey from the Census Bureau puts the middle Alpharetta house at 1996, give or take a year. Of the 26,223 homes in the city, 34.6 percent date from 1990 to 1999, 29.7 percent are older, and 16.1 percent were finished in 2010 or later. A single street can mix very different pipes and drywall.

A house of that vintage has had a long run on its water heater, washer hoses and roof, and UGA Extension's home checklist lists all three as things to examine in any house. In the city, 8,737 of 25,032 occupied homes (34.9 percent) are rented, so for many readers the first phone call goes to a landlord.

Rain does the rest. The National Weather Service's Atlanta station logged 63.18 inches in 2024 where a normal year brings 49.71, and Alpharetta's floodplain page says flash floods can lift streams and creeks out of their banks in a short time. A gauge reading across town may not match your address.

From your call to the first look

  1. You describe the house. A local contractor serving Alpharetta, Milton, Johns Creek, Cumming and Roswell answers and asks for the street address, the wet rooms, the time the water began and roughly how old the house is. Renters place the first call with the landlord or property manager.

  2. Next comes a time. The contractor offers a time to come and look. Until then, stay on dry ground and photograph everything before it moves.

  3. Then the look itself. A contractor can trace the water to its source, see how far it spread, read how wet the materials still are and lay out the options, such as drying a material where it lies or taking it out. What follows depends on what turns up.
