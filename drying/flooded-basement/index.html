Wet basements and crawl spaces near Alpharetta

Flooded Basement and Crawl Space, Alpharetta, GA

Did the water arrive with the rain, or did the sump pump simply stop? A flooded basement or crawl space in an Alpharetta house conceals plenty: damp stored boxes, soaked wall bases and a wet subfloor overhead. Once the lower level looks safe from the stairs, call __PHONE_DISPLAY__ and ask about a time for a look.

While it rains, the next day, the weeks after

  1. While it rains.

    Alpharetta's floodplain page says streams and creeks here can climb out of their banks in a short time, and the National Weather Service in Peachtree City says flash floods usually follow heavy rain by six hours or less. Stay upstairs.

  2. The next day.

    CDC says to assume a flooded home that has been shut up for several days has mold, and EPA's flood page puts the risk on wood, drywall and carpet left wet past the 24-hour mark.

  3. The weeks after.

    EPA says drying typically needs several days to weeks, and CDC says crawl spaces need proper drainage and ventilation to dry out. Johns Creek's floodplain page adds that flood losses fall outside a typical homeowners insurance policy, so only your own policy and insurer can say what applies.

Get out and get help if

  1. Outlets, a panel, cords or a furnace standing in water downstairs: keep off the stairs. Leave it to 911 or the electric utility, and let an electrician clear the wiring before power returns.
  2. Gas smell around a wet furnace or water heater: get well away from the house, touch no switch or flame, then use 911 or the gas utility's emergency number, 877.427.4321.
  3. A gas-powered pump or generator belongs outside, 20 feet or more from every door, window and vent. A carbon monoxide alarm that starts to sound means everyone gets out and 911 comes next.
  4. Sewage or floodwater downstairs: do not touch it, and children and pets stay upstairs. North Fulton sewer calls go to 404-612-3061; Forsyth County Water and Sewer takes them at 770-781-2160.

Where the water usually comes from

  • Steady rain that soaks the ground around the foundation
  • Gutters that overflow or downspouts that empty against the wall
  • A sump pump that quits or loses power, or a floor drain that clogs
  • A washer, water heater or pipe that fails down on the lower level
  • A crawl space with poor drainage and still air

What gets dried and what gets thrown out

Stored cardboard, carpet and the bottom foot of drywall are the first things to examine. CDC says items that floodwater touched and that cannot be disinfected get thrown away; drywall, rugs and cloth are its examples. In a crawl space the contractor checks ground cover, insulation and framing for dampness. A sump pump or drain that failed is a plumber's repair and sits apart from the drying.

Looks you can take from dry ground

Stay with looks that feel comfortable from dry ground. Leave panels, wires, standing water near cords and sewage alone.

  1. From the head of the stairs, note the water level and anything electrical close to it. Do not wade in.

  2. See where each downspout lets out; UGA Extension's checklist wants gutter water carried two to three feet from the house.

  3. From dry ground, notice whether the sump pump is running and whether the floor drain looks open.

  4. Photograph the watermark on the walls and on stored belongings before anything moves.

Four questions for the lower level

How deep did the water stand, and for how long? Ask how the walls and the subfloor above the wet area get checked, what becomes of stored items and carpet, and whether a plumber looks at the pump and drain first.

Rebuilding a finished lower level

Milton's residential page counts basement, attic and bonus-room finishes among permit jobs, and Johns Creek wants a word with Community Development before property in its regulated floodplain is altered. Ask first; the city-desk page lists each desk.
